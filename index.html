Executive summary

If you read only one part, read this.

    JTI's mandate is "fulfilling moments". Smoking gives pleasure in three ways: the five-minute moment, the five senses, and the feelings. The feelings are the "engine room". The physical side carries maybe 20 to 25% of it.
  • 02

  • 03

  • 04

  • 06

  • 07

  • 08

Fulfilling moments: where it all starts

JTI's company mandate is to "create fulfilling moments and a better future". The speaker opened by unpacking what a moment and fulfilment actually mean.

Pleasure needs a vehicle

All of us, and the animal kingdom too, seek pleasure. It is a coping mechanism for boredom and pressure. But pleasure needs either an activity (watching your football team) or something you use (chocolate, wine). Smoking is one such vehicle and it delivers in three ways: moments in time, the five senses, and the feelings.

The five-minute moment

  • A moment is simply the slice of time people are willing to give to smoking. For a cigarette, about five minutes.
  • Many smokers smoke almost by the clock: first coffee, then through the day. Some at fixed times (11 o'clock, 2 o'clock). During COVID lockdowns lots of smokers talked about going to the balcony around 4 or 5 pm every day.
  • Five minutes fits between almost anything: between two meetings, before the bus comes.
  • It is also long enough to recompose. The analogy used was a boxer losing a fight who gets one minute in the corner, comes back and wins. Here you get five.
  • "Whistle-stop of pleasure": you can smoke at any point of the day. Wine in the morning or an energy drink before bed looks wrong. Most categories are boxed into certain occasions and times; cigarettes are not.

How a fulfilling moment comes together

CircumstancesWhere I am, who with, what I'm doing, when, why. Changes all day.
Need stateDark grumpy morning: energise. Friday at the pub: connect with friends.
Opportunity to smokeThe need state is made better by smoking than not. Some go home early from a night out without cigarettes.
Pain pointsEvery pleasure has a cost. Here: health, cost, stigma and more. Most severe of any category.

How many moments are there?

Billions of consumers, but in the end we are all a little bit boring. Day routines look much the same everywhere, India or Geneva.

Routine moments
  • Starting the day (coffee or tea, getting ready)
  • Commute, from leaving home to starting work
  • Routine breaks at work, alone or with colleagues
  • After meals
  • Relaxing at home in the evening
  • Close of the day
Frequent non-routine moments
  • Stress
  • Boredom
  • Celebration, the "cigar moment" after a good presentation or client result
  • Socialising, which depends heavily on who you're with (family vs work friends)

Rituals vs routine acts

Brushing teeth or putting on socks are forgettable routine acts. Some sequences carry meaning, like a wedding ring. Smoking looks identical to a non-smoker every time, but smokers describe it as a soundtrack to the situation at that moment, like a song chosen for your mood. Same act, completely different role depending on the situation.

The ritual across the five senses

The physicality of the cigarette, from opening the pack to putting it out. JTI puts this at roughly 20 to 25% of the overall appeal.

1. Opening the pack

  • Nature smells. Like a bakery, coffee beans or fresh bread. Non-smokers enjoy walking into cigar and pipe shops for the smell.
  • Visual order. 20 sticks neatly packed, like an army parade.
  • Simplicity and tradition. Hundreds of years old and authentic. With AI and tech everywhere, people like stepping back to traditional things.
  • Touch. Like gardeners who love getting their hands on leaves.

2. Lighting it

  • Taming fire. Humans love fire (cooking, protection, light) but know it can hurt or kill.
  • The edge of smoking comes from here. Watch a smoker's panic if he drops one. Bruce Willis in Die Hard with a cigarette works; with a nicotine patch it doesn't.
  • Prometheus: holding fire in your hand, close to your lips.
  • Lighthouse: alone at a bus stop at night, lighting up lets the world know you're there.

3. The smoke

  • Serenity. A cigarette on a balcony in the Alps, watching smoke drift away.
  • Taste. Real tobacco taste only comes from a cigarette. Heat-not-burn doesn't get close. Like barbecue chicken vs oven chicken.
  • Body language. Happy smokers blow upwards, deliberate. Bad day, blowing at their feet.
  • Seeing the smoke matters. Smoking in a dark room would be pointless for many.
  • Breathing under stress. When stressed it's less about nicotine or taste and more about the slow inhale and exhale.

4. Holding it

  • Personalisation: "who am I". Marilyn Monroe's sultry hold, Bogart's cigarette hanging from the lip.
  • The shape is like a pencil or crayon, familiar from childhood, and you can carry on doing other things while holding it.
  • In the 60s and 70s tobacco companies actually segmented smokers by how they held cigarettes.
  • Some hide it behind their back between drags so no one sees.

5. Extinguishing it

  • Smokers say "let me kill this cigarette". There is satisfaction in stopping the clock on the five minutes: crushing, stomping, drowning.
  • It sends messages. Stub it out and leave when you walk in: I don't want to be here. Stub one out and light another when you arrive: I want to stay with you.

Why this matters for switching

When smokers move to heat-not-burn or vapes, all these sensorial delights they've had 15 times a day for most of their lives start to disappear. That is where the "little struggles" with RRP products come from, and why many don't simply take up vaping or heat-not-burn.

The feelings: the engine room

This is where most of the pull of smoking sits. Four feelings were covered.

Retreat

An accepted excuse to step away from people or situations. At a wedding where you know no one, you wave your pack and go outside. Walking around outside the office kicking stones looks odd; doing the same with a cigarette looks fine. Stigma is real and rising, but this "reason to get away" is something smokers value.

Connoisseurship

There's a narrative that all brands are the same and everyone buys on price. People are looking for cheaper cigarettes, because they've become so expensive. But smokers know more about smoking than about wine or chocolate: tobacco tastes, counterfeits, dry vs moist. Some pick unusual brands to stand out, others the same brand as everyone to belong. Doing something 15 times a day for life, you learn the nuances.

Social facilitation

The most important one, because this is where the category begins. Nobody starts by buying a pack alone at 18 or 19. It starts socially, a cigarette over a beer at university. Offering and lighting someone's cigarette is social currency. Smokers stand in a circle. Social smoking has nothing to do with nicotine or taste; it's body language.

Self-expression

The cigarette is an extension of who you are. The way Bruce Willis smokes tells you about the character; the way Marilyn Monroe smokes is very different.

The Pulp Fiction scene

Used to tie it all together. Uma Thurman smokes a manufactured cigarette, holding it suggestively and blowing smoke at John Travolta to get his attention. He avoids eye contact while rolling his own with real craft. Intrigued, she stubs hers out and asks for one of his. The moment he hands it over and lights it, they connect. Every element is in that one scene: holding, blowing, stubbing, sharing, lighting, and what each person's smoking says about them.

The speaker said this introduction is given to all new JTI joiners and agencies, so we understand what they mean when they talk about moments and pain points.

Q&A: switching behaviour and young adults

How do people change, given the ritual and the struggle?

  • Two broad types. Some want to keep smoking and look for a cigarette-like experience wherever they can. Others find the heat in the kitchen so bad they want to leave smoking behind completely and take up something new.
  • Market context shapes the route. In Japan, where smoking stayed normal for long, heat-not-burn worked as a "new way of smoking", especially for younger smokers. In the UK and US, stigma is so strong that people want nothing cigarette-like, so they go to vapes, pouches and new flavours.
  • Individual nature matters too. Exploratory people get far more from the RRP world (pouches, heat-not-burn, flavours). Cigarettes have many brands, but as a tool they are fairly bland. This is part of why the segmentation is being done.

Do younger generations feel the same?

  • Language rule: always say "young adults" / "younger adults" when talking about nicotine users. JTI will correct this in our writing; it's standard for every agency.
  • Young adults are moving towards vapes and pouches as a step away from "old-fashioned" smoking, and the data suggests they stay in RRP rather than moving on to cigarettes.
  • Even so, per JTI's tracker, cigarettes are still the biggest entry point into nicotine today. And many developing markets have no RRP at all, so the young vs old story depends on the market.

The 5W+H framework

JTI's structure for understanding consumers. Earlier they called it the 6Ws. For years questionnaires muddled topics across these buckets; now every question is placed deliberately.

BucketThe questionWhat sits under it
WhoWho are our consumers?Demographics, psychographics, behaviour (sole smokers, multi-category users, RRP soloists), segmentation, behavioural stages. Purely descriptive, doesn't explain why. Old focus was LA-24 new entrants and gender (pink packs vs black "macho" packs); the category is now far more nuanced. Differences need to be simple and differentiating.
WhyWhy do they use a product, and why do they change?Use: 5 consumption jobs (relax, energise, moment of joy, foster relationships, boredom). Category agnostic. Change: 5 pain points (health, price, stigma, inability to fit in, regulation). Price example: UK packs now cost close to £20. New study with ReWired on Forces of Progress. Years ago JTI assumed taste, pack or brand drove smoking; it turned out far more emotional.
WhereIn which moments?Usage situations, category agnostic. Smokers and vapers often use in the same places; a pouch works where you can't smoke. Moment list is now locked. Some markets used to have 35 moments, the panel had 12.
WhatWhat do they look for in our products?Equity (brand image) vs product (tobacco taste, satisfaction, ease of maintenance, new flavours). Kept at macro-driver level, not every feature. Some items changed after legal feedback.
HowWhere are they in behaviour change?Transtheoretical model: aware of a problem, consider, plan, trial, buy. Self-efficacy (how confident am I that I can change). Goal: know who has an awareness, consideration or planning problem and what in-store intervention helps. Links into shopper.
What's nextThe futureScenario planning. Out of scope for this project.

Framework fatigue

  • JTI has built framework after framework over the years: segmentations (13 segments, then 7), behavioural stages, Schwartz values, Ipsos Censydiam, jobs to be done. Explicit ask: don't create more frameworks from this research.
  • Until about 2 to 3 years ago, a JTI questionnaire could go out without a single question on moments. Now moments are seen as essential.
  • Why moments matter for switching: asking someone to quit and use heat-not-burn forever is scary. Asking them to replace one smoking moment with heat-not-burn, then more over time, is how change happens.
  • They want coverage as holistic as possible, within a limit on questionnaire length.

The old segmentation and what went wrong

The previous segments made sense on paper, but they did not connect to trade and were costly to use.

Traditional Confident

Older, around 55, heavy smoker. Focused on the positives, wants tobacco flavour, low pain points, not smoking for image or social reasons.

Image Seekers / Mindset

The "cool" younger, trendy adult, university age.

Smart Economizers

Looking for cheaper but better value, maybe a cheaper capsule.

Conscious

Those feeling the stigma.

Socializers

Defined by smoking mainly in social moments.

The core problem

Now how do I sell more cigarettes in store with that?

Why it didn't work

  • Built by throwing everything in. Drivers, pain points, life attitudes, lifestyles and moment circumstances all went into the algorithm, and statistics decided the clusters. Some segments ended up defined by moments, some by psychographics, some by a pain point. A mismatch.
  • Every behaviour sat in every segment. Sole smokers, multi-category users and even RRP soloists were spread across all segments, so it was hard to act on.
  • Too expensive to reuse. Long batteries were needed to recruit even for qual. Every study carried the cost.
  • Hard to explain. About 10 minutes per segment.
  • Felt Western. Pictures and lives were Western; developing markets couldn't relate.
  • Weak link to trade. This time JTI wants a much stronger connection between segmentation and trade.

Why segmentation is different in this category

Tobacco is a "dark" category. TV and most point-of-sale communication are banned, so segmentation is used heavily for media planning and communication, and activation has to be behaviour-led and pain-point-led. That is why the variables used will always differ from other FMCG categories.

The new segmentation design

Behaviour first. Full build detail is coming in the next session; this is the early thinking shared.

Layer 1: behavioural, explains category to category change

Based on what people actually use, plus their affinity to it: how much do I like my smoking, my heat-not-burn, or both if I'm multi-category. Consumers are on a behavioural journey.

~70 to 80% of nicotine users

Smokers

Many markets are still smoking-only. Even where RRP is everywhere, many choose to smoke despite the pain.

Hypothesis: split into those happy to keep smoking for good, and daily smokers who want to change at some point. Given the size, this group likely needs splitting a few ways.
Smoke + regular RRP

Multi-category users

Hypothesis: two types. Smoking lovers dragged into RRP for occasions where they can't smoke. And leavers, using RRP most of the time on the way out of smoking.
No longer smoke

RRP users

Heat-not-burn only, vape only, pouch only, and a small group mixing two or three.

Layer 2: psychographic, explains brand to brand choice

Brand groups liked the old psychographic view and want it back: show-offs vs those who want to fit in, excitement seekers vs conservative. This layer also carries brand equity and some shopper. Two questionnaires follow this split: a category questionnaire for Layer 1 and a sub-segment questionnaire for Layer 2.

The commercial test: the Ploom kiosk

JTI loses money every time it hands someone a Ploom device. It's an investment. But often the device ends up in a bin a week or two later. The segmentation has to tell them who to give it to. The ambition: shrink ~250 questionnaire variables into about 6 golden questions overall, and 4 to 5 for Layer 1. Someone walks into a kiosk, answers them, and staff know whether to hand over the device or not.

Must be

Simple and distinctive

Elevator test: explain segment 1 vs segment 2 between the 7th floor and the 1st.

Must be

Measurable and actionable

A few questions can classify someone, and the result tells you what to do.

Must be

Market-relevant

A global "red thread" skeleton. Markets add the meat on the bone, like demographics. Smokers in Japan are older than in Nigeria.

Forces of progress: where our pilot fits

The ReWired work uses forces of progress to explain switching:

Push

Pain points of smoking pushing you out.

Pull

Attraction of RRP: healthier, more flavours.

Anxiety

Worries about the new product: leaks in the bag, can't hold it properly, no nature smell when you open it.

Habit

The old ritual: lighting up, the inhale and exhale. Number of moments links to habit too.

JTI said this is the part Netscribes knows best, since the pilot analysis was built on hypotheses around push, pull, anxiety and habit, and Sharad confirmed it. JTI was open that forces of progress was included partly for internal reasons, because another part of the business sees it as the best lens. They described the approach as "belt and braces": both consumption jobs and forces of progress are covered in the questionnaire.

Supporting lists we'll get

  • Consumption jobs with descriptions (e.g. "getting ready for the challenge" when groggy and need to wake up focused = energise).
  • Switching jobs, recently updated with one more added.
  • Moments list, deliberately limited, chosen for a spread across jobs. Breaks at work lean towards relaxing; breaks with colleagues towards fostering relationships.
  • Product drivers list (taste, tobacco satisfaction, ease of maintenance and so on), adjusted after legal feedback.
  • Behaviour change battery: awareness, consideration, future intentions, trial, purchase, daily use. JTI stressed nothing in the questionnaire is aimless; every question has a purpose. They want our view on scales, linkages and what analysis is possible.

Sampling and weighting discussion

Our team raised how demographic weights would work across geographies, since the pilot had no weighting and a common framework is the goal.

What JTI confirmed

  • Within each market: a majority nationally representative sample of nicotine and tobacco users, plus boosts for low-incidence groups such as pouch users.
  • Across markets: roughly similar sample sizes, not proportional to population. India having several times more smokers than the UK won't mean several times the sample. Around 1,500 respondents per market was mentioned.
  • This is a full adult study run outside the panel, so all demographics are in the questionnaire. The pilot data we got likely lacked the panel's demographic fields.
  • Ipsos already has detailed incidence data for sampling. JTI can share detailed weights by category, gender, age and even sub-category if needed.
  • One framework across all markets. Some cells may be empty in some countries, but the framework won't change.

How weighting will be handled

  • No weighting while building the segmentation.
  • Once segments exist, weight up or down to population for sizing, so the business can see global counts in millions.
  • Our concern: without a plan for cross-country weighting at aggregation, there could be rework going back market by market. JTI agreed it's a fair point; the exact weighting approach (e.g. by respondent) is still to be discussed with Federico.
  • Suggestion made on the call: Federico to share an overview of the markets and incidences (combustible, multi-category, etc.) so we know who is being surveyed.

Timing mentioned

First batch of markets after November, the rest early next year (February to March). Results to cover all markets, with the global framework carried through and enough market specificity for local teams to use it.

What this means for us

Practical takeaways and open items for the Netscribes team.

Carry into our analysis

  • Layer 1 is behavioural and about category change. Our STRUGGLE and ease-of-progress thinking from the pilot sits naturally here, since it was built on push, pull, anxiety and habit.
  • Think early about variable reduction. The real deliverable is a small set of golden questions that classify people reliably, not a complex algorithm.
  • Every segment must pass the elevator test and be clearly different in behaviour, not a mix of everything.
  • Keep segment definitions market-agnostic; leave demographics as the market layer.
  • Use moments and consumption jobs as category agnostic across cigarettes, HnB, vapes and pouches.

Watch-outs

  • Always write "young adults" / "younger adults" in anything we send.
  • Don't introduce new frameworks. Map to 5W+H and the existing lists.
  • Don't repeat the old mistake of clustering on everything at once.
  • JTI asked for parts of this session to be treated carefully as they touched on sensitive topics. Keep this summary internal.

Open items to follow up

  • Agree the cross-market weighting approach for aggregation (who: Federico and JTI analytics).
  • Get the market list, incidences and booster plan.
  • Get the final consumption jobs, switching jobs, moments and driver lists.
  • Confirm we'll receive full demographics this time.

Prep for the next session

  • Walkthrough of both questionnaires (category and brand).
  • How JTI plans to build the Layer 1 segments.
  • Come with a view on scales, question linkages and feasible analysis.
  • Light background reading on forces of progress and the transtheoretical model.
